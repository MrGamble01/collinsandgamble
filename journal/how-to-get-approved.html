How to get approved
by the Influencer Program.

We can't accelerate Amazon's approval — nobody can — but we can tell you what we've observed about the bar it sets, what to put on the channels you submit, and the three patterns we see most often in the applications that get declined.

Category · Guide Reading time · 8 minutes By · The agency Published · 2026

Before anything else: this is the one part of the program we cannot help with. We sign approved Amazon Influencers; we do not get unapproved creators in. Amazon evaluates each application against its own criteria, on a timeline it controls. What we can offer is a practical read on what the bar appears to be — based on the applications we've seen succeed, the applications we've seen fail, and the patterns we've heard from creators who eventually got in after a no.

None of what follows is gospel. Amazon publishes minimal guidance, and the system likely changes. Treat this as the best-available read from people who watch it closely, not as a checklist with guaranteed outcomes.

What the program is actually looking for

The Amazon Influencer Program is, in Amazon's eyes, a content supply channel. The bar appears to be a single question, phrased many different ways across the application: will this person create content that helps Amazon shoppers decide to buy? Everything else is a proxy for that question.

That framing matters because it explains why follower count is less determinative than people assume. Amazon doesn't seem to be buying reach in the abstract; they appear to be buying the likelihood that an approved creator will actually produce videos that perform on product pages. Big followings on the wrong platform with the wrong engagement profile lose to smaller followings with clearer signal that the person makes buying-relevant content.

Amazon isn't buying reach. They appear to be buying the likelihood that you'll produce videos that perform on product pages.

The channels you submit, and what they should look like

At application time, Amazon asks you to nominate one or more social channels — typically Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, or Facebook — for them to evaluate. The choice matters. The state of those channels matters more.

  1. Pick the channel where you talk about products. Not the one with the biggest number. The one where the actual content most resembles what a shopper would find useful. If you have a TikTok with 80k followers that's mostly dance content and an Instagram with 12k followers that's mostly product reviews, submit the Instagram. The reviewer is looking for signal that you make buying-relevant content. The dance account doesn't send that signal even if the number is bigger.
  2. Have recent activity. An inactive channel — even a previously successful one — suggests you won't be active on the program either. Three to six months of consistent posting before you apply is a reasonable bar to clear.
  3. Show engagement, not just reach. Comments, saves, shares, and replies appear to matter more than impressions. If you have a smaller account where people ask questions, request links, and tag each other in your posts, that's a stronger signal than a larger account where nobody interacts.
  4. Make the product content findable. If the reviewer scrolls your last twelve posts and the first product mention is post number nine, the application is probably done already. Put recent product-relevant content at the top, even if it means delaying the application a few weeks to build it.

What gets people declined

Across the declined-applications stories we've heard from creators who eventually got in, three patterns come up so often they're worth naming.

Pattern one: the content doesn't look like commerce

A creator who posts beautiful lifestyle content but rarely names products, links to anything, or talks about purchases, is going to look ambiguous to the reviewer. Lifestyle content is fine — but if it's the dominant signal on your channel, the reviewer has to infer that you'll make buying-relevant content for Amazon, and inference loses to evidence.

The fix is not to abandon lifestyle content. It's to make sure that some recent fraction of your posts are explicitly product-named, product-linked, or product-reviewed in a way that a stranger could recognize within seconds. The reviewer is scanning. Help them scan.

Pattern two: the engagement doesn't match the reach

Follower counts have a complicated relationship with reality at this point, and the reviewer almost certainly knows it. A channel with a large follower count and very low engagement is either an old account that's gone dormant, a purchased audience, or a real audience that's no longer interested. None of those help Amazon predict that you'll perform on the program.

A smaller channel with proportionally strong engagement reads as cleaner signal. If your engagement-to-follower ratio is embarrassingly low for the size of the account, consider whether another channel — or a different application strategy — is the stronger move.

Pattern three: nothing connects the channel to Amazon

Some applications get declined for a more avoidable reason: the channel shows no engagement with Amazon as a platform. The reviewer is implicitly asking whether you'll be a productive participant on Amazon specifically, and a feed that never mentions Amazon, never links to Amazon, and never discusses Amazon products doesn't help that case.

You don't need to be an Amazon-only creator to clear this bar. But having a few recent posts that explicitly mention Amazon products — in a real, unforced way — helps the reviewer see the connection. Genuine affiliate links to existing products you've recommended, even outside the Influencer Program, are also a credible signal.

The reviewer is implicitly asking whether you'll be a productive participant on Amazon. Help them answer yes.

What the timeline actually feels like

Amazon's timeline for review is opaque and inconsistent. We've heard back times ranging from same-day to several months. Most of what we hear lands in the one-to-six-week range. There is no visible queue, no status page, and no useful follow-up mechanism beyond the application portal itself.

What we tell creators we're talking to is to expect a wait, not to plan around a date, and not to refresh the portal constantly. The application either succeeds, in which case you'll get an email, or it doesn't, in which case you'll usually get an email saying so. Re-applying after a decline is possible, but only after the channels have meaningfully changed. Re-applying the same week with the same evidence is not strategic.

If you get declined

A decline is not a verdict on you as a creator. It is, in the most useful interpretation, a signal that the channel you submitted didn't make a clear enough case for the kind of content Amazon's evaluating for. The honest move is to take the next three to six months and build the kind of evidence you'd want the reviewer to see — product-named posts, real engagement, occasional Amazon-relevant content — and then re-apply with a channel that tells a different story than the one that got declined.

Most successful re-applications we've heard about used a different primary channel, or the same channel after a meaningful content shift. Rare wins came from re-applying unchanged, and we'd bet those were closer-call applications the first time around.

If you get approved

The first thirty days after approval are the highest-leverage month of your time on the program. Your storefront URL is live. Your ability to upload on-site review videos is live. Most new creators take this month to set up an Instagram-style profile and then plateau. The ones we want on our roster — and the ones who do best independently — use this month to:

  • Set up the storefront's category structure (six to ten categories, named for shoppers, not for self-expression).
  • Populate the storefront with thirty to fifty starter products in categories you can speak to credibly.
  • Upload two to four on-site review videos onto product pages — products you actually own and can demonstrate honestly.
  • Get into a posting rhythm: a target of one or two on-site reviews per week, sustainable enough that you'll still be doing it in three months.

Approval is the start. The shape of your first month determines whether the program starts to compound or sits flat. Most creators don't get this right, which is the entire reason this agency exists. The storefront piece and the format piece cover the next steps in more detail.
