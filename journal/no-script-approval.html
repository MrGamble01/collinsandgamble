Why we won't
approve a script.

Every brand asks at some point. Some ask in the first call. Some ask politely in the contract negotiation. Some assume it's the default and are surprised when the question doesn't come up. The answer is no. The reasoning is mechanical, not philosophical.

Category · Stance Reading time · 7 minutes By · The agency Published · 2026

We turn down scripted-review work roughly twice a quarter. Not because we don't want the revenue — we do — but because the campaign would underperform every reasonable benchmark, and the brand would, fairly, hold us responsible. The cheaper conversation is the one we have up front. This is the long version of that conversation, in case the short one didn't quite land.

The format is the reason

An Amazon on-site review video lives inside a product listing, shown to shoppers who are already deciding whether to add to cart. We've written about the format in detail elsewhere; the relevant point here is that the shopper has already cleared the awareness stage on their own. They are not here to be sold to. They are here to make sure the thing on the page is the thing they thought it was. That is a different content problem than awareness advertising, and the answer to it is a different kind of creative.

Awareness advertising rewards polish, brand-coded language, and scripted clarity. Decision-stage content rewards the opposite. A shopper at the buy box doesn't want to be re-pitched the features they already read above the fold. They want a person they implicitly trust to confirm — or fail to confirm — that the product does the thing it says it does. The closer that confirmation feels to a real person making a real call, the more it converts. The closer it feels to a marketing department's preferred phrasing, the less it does.

Why shoppers can tell, even when they can't say

People who shop on Amazon have, on average, seen thousands of review videos in their lifetime. They have an unconscious model of what an honest review sounds like, what it looks like, and what the rhythm of it is — pauses where someone is thinking, small physical interactions with the product, asides that are slightly off-topic in the way real speech is. They also have an unconscious model of what a paid spot sounds like, and they recognize it within a few seconds. Not because they are cynical; because pattern recognition for "this person is reading something" is one of the most basic social skills a human has.

What this means in practice: a video that hits its marks verbally but feels off in any of the small ways scripts feel off — slightly too-fluent diction, no false starts, perfectly even pacing, a tonal smoothness that doesn't match the creator's other work — will lose the viewer's trust before the message lands. The viewer may not be able to articulate what was wrong. They will close the video and they will not click the buy button. The script earned the brand the wrong outcome on the wrong page.

What "honest" means operationally

"Honest" in our usage is not a synonym for reckless, unprepared, or unprofessional. The roster's creators show up prepared. We send a brief in advance of every send. The brief tells the creator what the product is for, who it's actually right for, which features actually matter, and which moments of the unboxing or demonstration are likely to be most interesting to a shopper at the buy box. We ask the creator to mention specific things if they're true (gift-boxed, dishwasher-safe, made in a specific place); we explicitly ask them not to mention things if they're not true.

What we don't do is write the language they're supposed to use. We don't dictate the structure of the video. We don't approve the take before it goes up. The creator films the video in their voice, with the brief in their head and the product in their hands. The result is recognizably the creator, recognizably the product, and recognizably an actual opinion. The components a brand-safety review would flag — claims of cure, comparisons to competitor brands, unsubstantiated performance numbers — we cover in the brief by clarifying what's accurate.

How we brief without scripting

A brief, in our practice, is one page. It has five sections.

  1. What the product is. One sentence in plain language. Not the marketing line — the functional description that would survive an editor's pass. The creator can write the marketing line if they want to. We don't put it in their mouth.
  2. Who it's right for. The actual user — described by context, not demographic. "The person who keeps the kitchen clean themselves" is a better brief than "ages 25–54." Helps the creator know who they're speaking to.
  3. The three features that actually matter. Out of all the things you could say, the three that drive the purchase. The creator decides which of those to lead with based on their feel for the audience.
  4. What's true that's worth mentioning. Real claims, in plain language. Materials, origin, warranty, ship-from, anything that survives a fact-check. The creator decides whether and how to mention them.
  5. What not to claim. Things the legal team would flag. Comparative performance numbers. Medical or safety claims that aren't substantiated. Anything the brand hasn't earned the right to say. The creator avoids these by knowing the boundary, not by reading around it.

The brief takes maybe twenty minutes for the creator to read. The video takes maybe ten minutes to film. The output is structured without being scripted, accurate without being defensive, and recognizably the creator's voice without being a free-for-all. That is the operational shape of "honest."

Brand safety, answered

The legitimate concern behind a script-approval request is usually not creative control. It's risk: the worry that the creator will say something false, defamatory, or brand-damaging, and that the brand will have to clean it up after the fact. That is a real worry. We answer it three ways.

First, the brief covers what the creator should and shouldn't say at the level of fact. The creator does not invent performance numbers because the brief tells them which numbers are real. They don't compare to competitors because the brief tells them not to. The brief is the brand-safety document; it does the work scripts are usually trying to do, with less of the tonal cost.

Second, we don't run brands whose products fail their own brief. If the product can't survive an honest unboxing, no script is going to fix that. Better to know now. We've turned down briefs from products we couldn't put on camera in good conscience, and we'd do it again.

Third, the contract with the creator covers what happens if a video says something materially false. The creator agrees to re-film. We agree to re-brief. The brand isn't left holding the bag for a creative miss — but the cleanup is mechanical, not litigative, and it almost never happens, because the brief usually does its job.

The brands we've turned down

Three categories show up most often in the script-approval request column. We've turned down work in each of them, more than once.

  • Health and supplement brands that want specific outcome language ("helps with X," "reduces Y") in the spoken script. We can't put those words in a creator's mouth in a way that survives FTC scrutiny. We can let the creator say what they actually felt, which is usually fine and often better.
  • Brands new to influencer work who treat the script as the deliverable rather than the conversion. Sometimes a conversation about the format gets us to a yes anyway; sometimes the brand wants the campaign they already imagined, and that's a fair preference — it just isn't a fit for what the on-site format does.
  • Brands with internal review processes designed for TV. Some creative teams are built around the legal-marketing-script-approval workflow that broadcast advertising requires. We can't slot into that workflow without making the work worse. A short conversation usually clarifies whether the brand can accommodate a different model.

What changes when we trust the creator

The creators on our roster are not amateurs improvising into a phone. They are people who have built audiences specifically around honest product takes, who have approval from Amazon to upload reviews directly onto product pages, and who get paid commission on the sales their videos drive. They have, in other words, a sharper incentive than any agency to produce a video that actually converts. When we trust them with the brief instead of the script, the videos perform better, the brands renew at higher rates, and the creators stay on the roster longer. The arithmetic isn't subtle.

"We won't approve a script" is, in the end, a small policy statement that says something larger about how we think the work should be done. We brief because we have to. We don't script because it doesn't work. The brands that have been with us longest are the ones who, after a campaign or two, stopped asking.
