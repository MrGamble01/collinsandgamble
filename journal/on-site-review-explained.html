The Journal Format

The on-site review video,
explained.

The format gets less scrutiny than it deserves. Everyone in the program talks about "the videos." Almost nobody breaks down what the format actually is — and what that mechanically demands from the creators who shoot them.

Category · Format Reading time · 9 minutes By · The agency Published · 2026

The Amazon on-site review video is a short video clip that plays inside an Amazon product detail page. It is the most consequential piece of content in the program, and it lives inside what is probably the most consequential page on the open internet — the page where shoppers actually decide whether to add to cart. Everything else the program offers (the storefront, the commission engine, the brand deals) orbits around that one piece of content. So it is worth understanding precisely what it is, what it isn't, and what that distinction asks of you when you make one.

What the format actually is

Mechanically, an on-site review video is an MP4 between roughly ten seconds and just under a minute, recorded by an approved Amazon Influencer Program creator and uploaded directly to a specific product listing — not to YouTube, not to a creator's profile, but to the listing's review carousel. The upload is associated with one or more ASINs (Amazon's product identifiers) and a thumbnail. Once published, the video joins a small set of other customer videos on that product's page, where Amazon's review section surfaces it to shoppers who scroll past the gallery, the price box, and the bullets into the review area below.

That is a tight specification, and most of the format's behavior falls out of it. The video lives on the listing, which means the shopper has already done the work of arriving at the page. The video plays after the buy box is in view, which means the shopper has already absorbed the price and the photos. The video is among other videos, which means there is competition for the click on the thumbnail. And the video is recorded specifically for that product, which means the entire framing assumes the viewer is asking one specific question: should I buy this thing.

The entire framing assumes the viewer is asking one specific question. Every editorial choice in the format follows from there.

Where it lives, exactly

On a typical desktop layout, the customer videos surface in a "Videos" carousel below the product description and reviews summary, often labeled with something like "Customer Videos" or "Videos for this product." On mobile — which is where most of the traffic is — the videos appear inline closer to the buy box, and the first few often display as larger thumbnails with a play indicator. Amazon has historically experimented with placement (sometimes higher, sometimes lower; sometimes auto-rotating, sometimes static) and we assume they will keep experimenting. The point is not the pixel location. The point is that the video is part of the listing itself, not a destination the shopper has to leave the page to reach.

This is the structural advantage that separates the on-site review from every other creator format. A YouTube review pulls the viewer off the listing into YouTube; even when it converts, the path back to the buy button is fragile. A TikTok review competes for attention in a feed of unrelated content; the shopper has to be re-acquired, re-routed, and re-convinced. The on-site review never lets the shopper out. They watched it because they were already there. They buy it because the video closed the last objection between them and the cart.

What Amazon ranks (and what we infer)

Amazon does not publish the formula it uses to order videos in the carousel. What we have observed, across the campaigns we've run and the creators we represent, is that several signals appear to matter: completion rate (how many shoppers watch the video to the end), engagement-after-play (whether viewers click through to the storefront or buy the product), recency (newer videos can leapfrog older ones), and quality heuristics we can't see directly but appear to penalize obviously low-effort uploads (blurry stock, no audio, generic VO).

None of those signals are gameable in interesting ways, and that's mostly a feature. The honest, well-framed video by the creator who actually opened the box tends to surface. The drive-by, watermarked re-upload tends to sink. Amazon's incentives are aligned with the shopper's: the platform wants the video that converts, not the video that performed on another platform.

What creators control

A creator has direct control over a tight handful of variables. Understanding what's in their hands and what isn't is the difference between treating the format like a craft and treating it like a lottery.

  • Length. Anywhere from about ten seconds to just under sixty. We've found that videos in the 25–45 second range tend to perform best — long enough to demonstrate the product, short enough to keep completion rates honest.
  • Thumbnail. The first frame matters more than any other choice. It is the only thing standing between the shopper and the click. Hold the product clearly in frame for that opening moment.
  • Aspect ratio and orientation. Vertical or square works better than landscape on mobile, and mobile is where the audience is. Landscape video gets letterboxed and the thumbnail goes flat.
  • Audio. Speak. Even if you script nothing, narrate what you're showing. Mute-watchers exist, but they're a minority on a buying-intent surface.
  • The first three seconds. If you don't hook in the first beat — name the product, hold it up, show the use — the shopper scrolls. The carousel is forgiving in one direction (you keep them) and ruthless in the other (you lose them).
  • Content honesty. Demonstrate the actual feature you say it has. Show the unboxing if there's an unboxing moment. Show the use case if there's a use case. Amazon shoppers can smell stock footage, and they bounce.

What creators don't control

The unromantic half of the spec: a creator cannot pick where their video appears in the carousel, who sees it, or when it surfaces. They cannot promote the video the way they would a YouTube upload — there is no equivalent of subscribers, comments, or a public feed. They cannot guarantee that an upload will appear immediately; Amazon's review pipeline can take hours to days. And they cannot edit a video after it ships in the way they could re-upload a YouTube revision. The platform owns the distribution surface entirely. The creator owns the content and the cadence.

This is, again, mostly fine. The platform's control of the distribution surface is what makes the buying intent so dense and the conversion so possible. A creator trading some of the autonomy of a feed-based platform for proximity to the buy button is making, on the whole, an excellent trade.

The platform owns the distribution surface. The creator owns the content and the cadence. On Amazon, that's an excellent trade.

What this means for creators

Treat the format as a discipline, not a piece of content. The craft is not in any single video; it is in the rhythm of many. We push our roster toward three habits.

  1. Cadence over polish. Two uploads a week, every week, will outperform one perfect-looking video a month. The platform rewards recency. The shopper rewards specificity. Both of those compound only if you keep showing up.
  2. Thumbnail discipline. Treat the first frame like a print ad. Product centered. Face if that's your brand. Hold it for half a second before the action starts. A great video with a vague thumbnail will lose to a merely-good video with a clear one.
  3. Length is a budget, not a target. Pick the minimum number of seconds your demonstration actually needs. If you can show what you mean to show in twenty-two seconds, twenty-two is the right length. The carousel rewards completion, not effort.

What this means for brands

For a brand, the on-site review video is the closest thing the program offers to a guaranteed-relevant placement. Every viewer already chose your product page. Every play already cleared the hardest qualifying signal in any advertising channel: they wanted to be there. What you are buying is the chance to convert someone who is already considering the purchase. That is categorically different from buying impressions on a feed.

It also explains why honest reviews convert better here than polished ones. The shopper has already taken the marketing premise on board; they're past the awareness stage; they don't need to be re-pitched. What they need is one more piece of evidence that the thing on the page is the thing they thought it was. That evidence is almost always strongest from someone whose first take is recognizably uncoached.

The summary, written shorter

The format is a short video, on a specific product page, made by an approved creator, ranked by what shoppers actually do with it. It lives at the only moment that matters to the listing. It rewards cadence, honesty, and the discipline of the first three seconds. It punishes script-feel, vague thumbnails, and stale uploads. Treat it like a craft and the program returns the favor.
